You Cannot Train Your Way Out of a Hiring Freeze

James Tuttiett - 17 November 2026 - 5 min read

Alan Milburn's review of young people and work, published today, is the most serious attempt in a generation to deal with youth unemployment. It deserves a fair hearing. It also needs a warning attached: we cannot train our way out of a hiring freeze.

Most of today's coverage will focus on benefits. The more important sentence is in chapter three: "We have to stop treating youth employment principally as a problem of supply when it is also a problem of demand." That is the right diagnosis. The question is whether the remedy is big enough to match it.

The first rung is disappearing

Nearly one million young people in the UK are not in education, employment or training. That is one in eight. Six in ten have never had a job, and more than seven in ten say they want to be in work, education or training. This is not a generation that lacks ambition. It is a generation that lacks a way in.

The review's own figures show why. Entry-level vacancies have fallen by 49% over the past decade, and over the past year they fell 1.6 times faster than the wider labour market. In the review's words: "It is the bottom of the ladder being taken away."

When Reuters asked me about this last week, I put it this way: "You have this silent hiring freeze where AI can increasingly do a lot of jobs that exist in white-collar work." Add higher minimum wages and higher employer National Insurance, and the business case for taking a chance on someone with no experience gets weaker every year. At FDM we now train people for longer before we place them with clients, because the bar for a first role has moved.

What the review gets right

  • It names demand, not just skills. For decades, policy has focused on making young people more employable and assumed the jobs would follow. The review says plainly that they have not.
  • It puts money behind hiring. Employers would receive £3,000 for hiring a young person who has been out of work or education for six months, with £2,000 premiums for those facing greater barriers and for small businesses, and £2,500 per apprenticeship. That is around £1.1 billion a year, expected to create 170,000 additional jobs and apprenticeships.
  • It changes what schools are for. It calls for "aptitudes alongside knowledge", and for assessment that tests "judgement, problem-solving and practical competence" in a world shaped by AI.
  • It backs apprenticeships, with a path to 270,000 under-25 starts a year by 2036.

This is a step in the right direction.

Where it falls short

The gap is in the arithmetic. The review's own modelling suggests 500,000 additional employment and education opportunities may be needed by 2036. The incentive package creates 170,000.

Compare what others spend. The UK spent around 0.1% of GDP on all employment policies in 2025/26. The Netherlands spends around 0.3% on hiring incentives and subsidies alone, and Denmark around 1%. Matching the Dutch would mean roughly £6 billion more a year. The £1.1 billion package is welcome, but it is a fraction of what the countries we are measuring ourselves against invest.

The most powerful cost lever, employer National Insurance, is deferred until "fiscal conditions allow" and targeted at 21 to 24 year olds in retail and hospitality. Those sectors matter: they provide 37% of jobs for 16 to 24 year olds. But the roles AI is quietly removing are entry-level office, operations and technology jobs: the junior analyst, the graduate developer, the operations assistant. Nothing in the package is aimed at them.

Training without jobs is wasted money

The core issue is simple. There is no point investing in training if companies cannot create the jobs at the end of it.

A trained young person without a first job is still out of work. Worse, they have done everything asked of them and been met with what the review calls "a wall of silence" from employers. That teaches the wrong lesson about effort and reward, and it is the fastest way to turn a temporary gap into long-term detachment.

Supply and demand have to move together. Right now the plan to improve supply is far more developed than the plan to create demand.

Teach people to ask better questions

The review is right to shift schools from qualifications alone towards aptitudes. I would be more specific. In an AI-assisted workplace, routine knowledge work is the part that gets automated first. What stays valuable is critical thinking and inquisitiveness: the ability to question an answer, spot what is missing and ask the next question. Those are the skills that make a junior hire productive alongside AI rather than replaced by it.

Employers are saying the same. Only 3% of those who recruited 16 year olds into their first job said they lacked literacy or numeracy. What they lacked was work readiness, maturity and confidence.

But better skills only pay off if someone hires the person who has them.

What would make the difference

  1. Size the incentives to the gap. 170,000 opportunities against a need of 500,000 is not enough. The review itself argues for applying the same investment logic to human capital that we apply to infrastructure and energy. The hiring budget should reflect that.
  2. Extend incentives to entry-level white-collar and technology roles, where AI is removing the first rung fastest, not only retail and hospitality.
  3. Commit to the National Insurance relief now, with a date. Employers plan hiring a year or more ahead. Uncertainty is itself a cost.
  4. Reward UK job creation. Target support at employers who create new entry-level roles in the UK, weighted towards the regions with the highest rates of young people out of work, so the benefit reaches the whole country rather than concentrating where jobs are already plentiful.
  5. Ask employers to redesign entry roles for the AI era, not remove them. That is business's side of the bargain, and the review's First Chance commitment is the right place to start.

The bottom line

Milburn has shown that young people are not the problem. Most of them want to work. The question now is whether there will be enough jobs for them to walk into.

Training is the supply side. Jobs are the demand side. The UK needs both, and at the moment it is only seriously investing in one.
