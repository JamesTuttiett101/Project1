You Cannot Train Your Way Out of a Hiring Freeze

James Tuttiett - 17 November 2026 - 5 min read

Alan Milburn's review of young people and work, published today, sets out a ten-year plan covering schools, health, welfare and employer incentives. I support most of it. My concern is that it does far more to prepare young people for work than to create the jobs they will need.

Most of today's coverage will focus on benefits. The sentence I would point to is in chapter three: "We have to stop treating youth employment principally as a problem of supply when it is also a problem of demand." I agree with that diagnosis. The question is whether the remedy is big enough to match it.

The first rung is disappearing

Nearly one million young people in the UK are not in education, employment or training, one in eight of those aged 16 to 24. Six in ten have never had a job, and more than seven in ten say they want to be in work, education or training.

Entry-level vacancies have fallen by 49% over the past decade, and over the past year they fell 1.6 times faster than the wider labour market. The review describes this as "the bottom of the ladder being taken away."

When Reuters asked me about this last week, I said: "You have this silent hiring freeze where AI can increasingly do a lot of jobs that exist in white-collar work." Higher minimum wages and higher employer National Insurance add to the effect, because each increase makes it harder for an employer to justify hiring someone with no experience over someone with some. At FDM we now train people for longer before we place them with clients, because the bar for a first role has moved.

What the review gets right

The review accepts that demand is part of the problem. In its own words, public policy has for too long "focused on supply-side measures and ignored the demand side", assuming the market would create the jobs. Milburn says plainly that it has not.

It also puts money behind hiring. Employers would receive £3,000 for taking on a young person who has been out of work or education for six months, a further £2,000 for young people facing greater barriers and another £2,000 for businesses with fewer than 50 staff, plus £2,500 per apprenticeship. The package costs around £1.1 billion a year and is expected to create 170,000 additional jobs and apprenticeships.

On schools, it calls for "aptitudes alongside knowledge" and for assessment that tests "judgement, problem-solving and practical competence" as AI changes the work young people will do. It also sets a path to 270,000 apprenticeship starts a year for under-25s by 2036. I would back all of this.

Where it falls short

The review's own modelling suggests 500,000 additional employment and education opportunities may be needed by 2036. The incentive package would create 170,000 jobs and apprenticeships, about a third of that.

Comparable countries spend more. The UK spent around 0.1% of GDP on all employment policies in 2025/26. The Netherlands spends around 0.3% on hiring incentives and subsidies alone, and Denmark around 1%. The review estimates that matching the Dutch would cost roughly £6 billion more a year, more than five times the £1.1 billion proposed.

Employer National Insurance is the biggest cost lever available, and the review defers any cut until "fiscal conditions allow". When it comes, it covers 21 to 24 year olds and starts in retail and hospitality, which together provide 37% of jobs for 16 to 24 year olds. In my experience at FDM, the entry-level roles AI is cutting fastest are in offices: junior analysts, graduate developers and operations assistants. The package has nothing aimed at them.

Training without jobs is wasted money

There is no point investing in training if companies cannot create jobs for the people who complete it.

A young person who finishes a course and cannot find a first job is still counted as out of work. The review found that unsuccessful applicants often get no response at all, which it calls "a wall of silence". It also found that most young people who are NEET are economically inactive rather than looking for work. Training that ends in repeated rejection risks moving more of them into that second group.

Supply and demand have to move together, and at present the plan for supply is much further developed than the plan for demand.

Teach people to ask better questions

The review wants schools to build aptitudes as well as qualifications. I would name two: critical thinking and inquisitiveness. In an AI-assisted workplace, routine knowledge work is automated first. A junior hire who can question an answer, notice what is missing and ask the next question is useful alongside AI tools, and that work is much harder to automate.

Employers' own feedback points the same way. Only 3% of those who recruited 16 year olds into their first job said they lacked literacy or numeracy. They cited experience, maturity and confidence instead. Those qualities still depend on someone being willing to hire the young person in the first place.

What would make the difference

  1. Size the incentives to the gap. Funding 170,000 opportunities against a need of 500,000 leaves most of the shortfall uncovered. The review argues for applying the same investment logic to human capital that we apply to infrastructure and energy, and the hiring budget should follow that argument.
  2. Extend incentives to entry-level office and technology roles, where AI is reducing the number of junior positions, as well as retail and hospitality.
  3. Set a date for the National Insurance relief. Employers plan hiring a year or more ahead, and an undated commitment does not change those plans.
  4. Reward UK job creation. Target support at employers who create new entry-level roles in the UK, weighted towards the regions with the highest rates of young people out of work, so the benefit reaches the whole country.
  5. Ask employers to redesign entry roles around AI tools so that those roles keep existing. The review's First Chance commitment, which invites large employers to publish their own targets, including where possible 10% of entry-level vacancies for young people who are NEET, is the place to start.

When the Government responds to the review, the test I will apply is whether its hiring budget moves closer to the 500,000 figure in Milburn's own modelling.
